    How the numbers are made

    The data. All from Statistics Canada, downloaded automatically through its Web Data Service; each chart links to its table. Most series come from the Labour Force Survey (LFS), a monthly survey of about 65,000 households, and are seasonally adjusted unless the chart says otherwise. Job vacancies come from the Job Vacancy and Wage Survey, published about two months after the LFS.

    Margin of error. The LFS is a survey, so its monthly changes are estimates. This page calls a change statistically significant when it is more than 1.645 standard errors (90% confidence), using the standard errors Statistics Canada publishes. Statistics Canada’s own releases use a looser 68% level, one standard error.

    What moved the unemployment rate. Since 1 − the unemployment rate = employment ÷ (population × participation rate), each change splits into a jobs part, a population part and a participation part (log changes, scaled to percentage points; they add up to the actual change). Computed from the counts, not the rounded published rates. The panel shows the change from the previous month, with its axis capped at ±1 point so that the pandemic months don’t flatten the rest.

    Provinces. “3-mo trend” is the average rate of the last three months minus the average of the three months before (e.g. July–September against April–June). Provincial samples are small, so a single month’s change is noisy. The count at the top compares the published rates, to one decimal, with the month before.

    Industries. The margin of error for the year to date is not published. It is estimated from the published monthly and 12-month margins: LFS households stay in the survey for six months, so the error of a change grows with the gap between the two months until the samples stop overlapping. January uses the monthly margin, June onwards the 12-month margin, and the months in between a straight line in the variance (the squared error).

    Who is unemployed. Age and education groups come from table 14-10-0019 (not seasonally adjusted). Each month’s rate is computed from the counts, then averaged over 12 months, then ranked against every month since January 2000, following Gad Levanon’s Labor Matters. 45 to 54 is 25 to 54 minus 25 to 44; all ages (15 to 64) adds up 15 to 24, 25 to 54 and 55 to 64, counting a part Statistics Canada suppresses (fewer than 1,500 people) as zero. Groups averaging under 10,000 unemployed are not shown. Percentiles are coloured in ten steps of ten (blue below 50, orange above), rates in five steps.
